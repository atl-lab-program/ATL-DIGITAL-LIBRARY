Diabetes: Categories, Symptoms & Age of Onset

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic condition characterized by consistently high blood sugar (glucose) levels. It occurs when the body either does not produce enough insulin—a hormone made by the pancreas that lets glucose enter cells for energy—or cannot effectively use the insulin it produces.

Main Types Of Diabetes

• Type 1 Diabetes: An autoimmune reaction where the body mistakenly attacks and destroys insulin-producing beta cells in the pancreas. It usually develops in children, teens, or young adults, though it can appear at any age. People with Type 1 diabetes must take insulin every day to survive.

• Type 2 Diabetes: The most common form (accounting for roughly 90–95% of cases). The body becomes resistant to insulin, and over time, the pancreas cannot make enough insulin to keep blood sugar at normal levels. It is strongly linked to genetics, excess body weight, and physical inactivity, and is increasingly seen in younger adults as well as older populations.

• Gestational Diabetes: Develops during pregnancy in women who did not previously have diabetes. Hormonal changes block insulin's action. While it usually resolves after childbirth, both the mother and child face a higher risk of developing Type 2 diabetes later in life.

• Prediabetes: A warning stage where blood sugar levels are higher than normal, but not yet high enough for a Type 2 diabetes diagnosis. Lifestyle changes at this stage can often prevent or delay progression.

Common Symptoms

Symptoms often develop rapidly in Type 1 diabetes, whereas in Type 2 they may appear gradually or go unnoticed for years:

• Increased thirst (polydipsia) and frequent urination (polyuria)

• Unexplained weight loss (more common in Type 1)

• Increased hunger (polyphagia)

• Persistent fatigue and weakness

• Blurred vision

• Slow-healing cuts, sores, or frequent infections (such as skin or yeast infections)

• Tingling, numbness, or pain in the hands or feet (more common in Type 2)

Potential Long-Term Complications

If blood sugar remains elevated over many years, it can damage blood vessels and nerves throughout the body, leading to:

  • Cardiovascular disease: Increased risk of heart attack, stroke, and high blood pressure.
  • Neuropathy (Nerve damage): Numbness, tingling, or burning pain, typically starting in the toes and feet.
  • Nephropathy (Kidney damage): Can progress to chronic kidney disease or kidney failure.
  • Retinopathy (Eye damage): Damage to blood vessels in the retina that can lead to vision loss or blindness.
  • Foot complications: Poor circulation combined with nerve damage increases the risk of ulcers and serious infections.
